    What a Cheriyal piece signals

    A Cheriyal piece signals itself at a glance.

    A red ground. A crowned figure. Wide almond eyes.

    Read the mask

    01 · Crown

    Nayaka-style crowns

    Collector Jagdish Mittal links the crowns to Nayaka painting of about 1540.

    02 · Eyes

    Large almond eyes

    The same Nayaka link covers the eyes: a signature most viewers would recognise.

    03 · Ground

    The red ground

    Scrolls are documented on a red ground, with blue, green, yellow, black and white.

    04 · Dot rows

    White dot rows

    The palette's white is documented as coming from river shells.

    Signals of status

    Official recognition is a symbol too.

    Lore vs evidence

    The colour code is lore. The rest can be checked.

    Popular colour code unverified

    • Blue: male gods
    • Green: Hanuman
    • Yellow: goddesses
    • Pink: ordinary people
    • Brown: demons

    Sellers' claim. Not found in any scholarly or journalistic source read for this report.

    What is documented

    • Red ground, with blue, green, yellow, black and white
    • Nayaka-style crowns and eyes, c. 1540 (Mittal)
    • GI certificate, 6 Oct 2010, no. 142

    The people

    Who holds the craft together?

    • Imagined communities
    • Family as deep root
    • The cultural iceberg

    Concept 2 · Imagined communities

    Three parties, one story.

    Nakashi painters made scrolls for hereditary bards, who performed caste origin stories for a patron community.

    Storyteller ↔ patron, as documented

    Each community had its own bard.

    Five pairings confirmed in the sources Two more reported pairings stay unconfirmed. Terms as the sources use them.

    The painted scroll

    The only archive.

    Scrolls hold narratives that were otherwise only oral.

    Old scrolls were ritually immersed in a river. The archive was built to be perishable.

    Illustration in the Cheriyal manner, not a documented scroll

    Hereditary transmission

    Skill runs down a family line.

    The most visible documented lineage is Dhanalakota. The craft passes from parent to child.

    2013

    The lineage moves to Hyderabad.

    Sai Kiran moved from Cheriyal to Boduppal, Hyderabad, in 2013. Reason given: with no storytellers, orders for scrolls became rare.

    Non-hereditary

    Apprentices carry the craft too.

    Apprenticeship by non-hereditary makers is documented. Ganesh argues four- or five-day workshops cannot transmit real skill.

    The gap in the record

    Women's roles: under-documented.

    Bose (2021) found GI authorised-user lists excluded piece-rate women trainees.

    Insight Naming a maker can make attribution easier to check.

    1/6Above the waterline

    What a buyer sees

    A scroll, a mask, a set of colours. Finished, framed and priced.

    The question for this scene: what holds them up?

    2/6Below the waterline

    What holds it up

    • Knowledge What makers and performers know: stories, conventions, recipes.
    • Skills What hands can do: preparing, shaping, grinding, painting.
    • Relationships Who the work depends on: tellers, patrons, households, suppliers.

    3/6Follow one object down · the scroll

    A scroll is a commission between people

    1. K

      Performers set the story's order and iconography; painters shape materials and style.

    2. S

      Khadi cloth is primed with rice starch, white clay, boiled tamarind seed and lac.

    3. R

      Hereditary performing rights tie a storyteller to a patron community.

    4/6Follow one object down · the mask

    A mask carries a household's method

    1. K

      The towering crowns and large eyes follow a visual convention one collector links to Nayaka painting, c.1540.

    2. S

      Sawdust and tamarind paste over a clay mould, then cotton, rice starch, white clay and tree lac.

    3. R

      Skill passes through households: one artist learned from his parents, and GI listing ran through kin and apprentices.

    5/6Follow one object down · the colours

    Colour is labour you cannot see

    1. K

      Bold colour, simple drawing and large figures were suited to being seen outdoors, from a distance.

    2. S

      Mineral pigments ground by hand, bound with tamarind and lac.

    3. R

      Some colour powders are bought in from Rajasthan, so supply is a relationship too.

    6/6The whole picture

    The surface moves. The base stays.

    “The medium might change, but the creative process and the use of natural colours remain the same.”
    Evidence
    Each of the nine points below the line is sourced.
    Our reading
    The iceberg, the three layers and the links between them are our framing. No source uses an iceberg.

    Whether buyers value what sits below the line.
